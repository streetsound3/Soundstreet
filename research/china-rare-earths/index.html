FLASH NOTE · TRADE · CHINA

China’s rare earth deadline could reignite a global supply squeeze

November 3, 2026 · Parker Onacilla

Summary. China’s suspension of expanded rare earth export controls expires on November 10, 2026, potentially restoring restrictions on additional rare earth elements, processing technologies, and certain foreign made products containing Chinese-origin materials. I believe this creates a meaningful supply chain risk as China accounted for 91% of global refined magnet rare earth production and 94% of permanent magnet production in 2024, according to the IEA. Bearish on rare earth dependent manufacturers and bullish on non-Chinese rare earth producers over the next 6–12 months.

Our call: Bearish on rare earth dependent manufacturers and bullish on non-Chinese rare earth producers. Tracked as Call #005.

What happened

  • April 4, 2025: China introduced export licensing requirements for 7 medium and heavy rare earth elements: samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium, and yttrium. The restrictions cover related materials and products.
  • October 9, 2025: China announced 6 additional export-control measures through Announcements 55, 56, 57, 58, 61, and 62. Five additional elements were included: holmium, erbium, thulium, europium, and ytterbium. Announcement 61 introduced licensing requirements for certain foreign-made products containing Chinese-origin rare earths when those materials represent at least 0.1% of the covered product’s value. This applies to specified products (not every foreign-made product). Other measures covered certain rare earth processing equipment, materials, and technologies.
  • October 30, 2025: Presidents Trump and Xi met in Busan, South Korea, and reached a trade understanding involving rare earth exports. The White House subsequently described China’s commitment to suspend the expanded controls.
  • November 7, 2025: China’s Ministry of Commerce issued Announcement 70, suspending all 6 October announcements until November 10, 2026. If the suspension is not extended, the additional controls could resume after that date.
  • November 9, 2025: Announcement 72 suspended the second clause of China’s December 2024 dual-use export restrictions on the US until November 27, 2026. This clause concerns gallium, germanium, antimony, superhard materials, and graphite. The separate prohibition on exports to US military users or for military purposes remains in place.
  • September 28, 2026: China announced an extension of joint US-China economic and trade arrangements through January 10, 2027. However, the announcement did not explicitly mention rare earth export controls, leaving the November 10 suspension’s status unresolved.

Note: The April 2025 controls on 7 elements remain in force. November 10 concerns the additional October 2025 measures, not every Chinese rare earth export restriction.

Why it happened

National security. China identifies rare earths as strategically important materials used in defense and advanced technologies. Its export-control announcements cite national security, national interests, and non-proliferation obligations.

Trade leverage. Rare earths give China significant negotiating power because foreign manufacturers depend heavily on Chinese refining and magnet production.

Industrial dominance. China has developed extensive mining, refining, and magnet manufacturing infrastructure. The IEA estimates that China accounted for 60% of mined magnet rare earth production in 2024, alongside its much larger shares of refining and magnet production.

How it flows through

Rare earths move through stages: mining, separation, refining, metal production, alloying, and permanent magnet manufacturing. Restrictions can impact stages of this supply chain.

  • Supply. Stricter licensing can delay or prevent shipments of certain materials and products. Manufacturers relying on Chinese-made magnets are particularly exposed.
  • Costs. Reduced availability can increase prices for rare earth oxides, metals, and magnets. Manufacturers may pay premiums to secure alternative suppliers.
  • Production. Companies unable to obtain necessary materials may reduce production, delay orders, or temporarily shut down facilities. The IEA reported that China’s April 2025 controls forced some automakers to reduce production or temporarily halt operations.
  • Profitability. Higher material costs can reduce profit margins, particularly when manufacturers cannot pass those costs to customers.
  • Long term effects. Manufacturers have greater incentives to diversify suppliers and invest in domestic processing and magnet production.

Exposure scale: The IEA estimates that full implementation of the expanded controls could put $6.5 trillion in annual downstream production outside China at risk. This represents the economic value of potentially exposed production, not predicted financial losses.

Names to watch

MP Materials MP

▲ Potential winner

Could benefit from manufacturers seeking US-based rare earth suppliers and domestic magnet production.

  • Operates Mountain Pass, a major US rare earth mining and processing operation.
  • Produced 2,599 metric tons of neodymium-praseodymium (NdPr) oxide in 2025, a 101% increase from 2024, according to its February 26, 2026 earnings announcement.
  • Produced its first NdFeB permanent magnets on commercial equipment at its Independence facility in 2025.

Lynas Rare Earths LYC

▲ Potential winner

Has established mining and processing operations outside China, positioning it to serve manufacturers seeking alternative supplies.

  • Operates the Mt Weld mine in Australia and a major processing facility in Malaysia.
  • Achieved its first production of separated dysprosium and terbium in 2025.
  • Its 2025 annual report identifies it as the only commercial producer of separated heavy rare earth products outside China.

MP offers exposure to US-based mining and magnet manufacturing, while Lynas has established international mining and processing operations. Both face execution, production capacity, and pricing risks.

What would prove us wrong

The November 10 deadline creates meaningful supply-chain risk for manufacturers and potential opportunities for alternative suppliers. 4 developments could weaken this thesis:

  • China extends the suspension. A formal extension or confirmation that the September 28 agreement covers rare earth controls would reduce immediate supply concerns.
  • Exports continue normally. If China continues approving licenses without significant delays or additional restrictions, manufacturers could avoid major disruptions.
  • Alternative supplies increase. Larger inventories, long-term contracts, recycling, and additional production outside China could reduce dependence on Chinese suppliers.
  • US-China relations improve. A new trade agreement could reduce the likelihood of further restrictions and restore confidence in international supply chains.

Our call: Bearish on rare earth dependent manufacturers and bullish on non-Chinese rare earth producers over the next 6–12 months.

I expect China’s November 10 deadline to create renewed supply-chain uncertainty, with the greatest downside for automakers, defense contractors, wind energy companies, and electronics manufacturers dependent on Chinese rare earth magnets. I expect MP Materials and Lynas to benefit from increased demand for alternative suppliers, although production capacity will limit how quickly they can capture that demand.

My call would change if China formally extends the suspension through January 10, 2027, or demonstrates that export licenses will continue to be approved without meaningful delays.

We track this as Call #005: MP Materials (MP) and Lynas Rare Earths (LYC), equally weighted, beat the S&P 500 from November 3, 2026 to November 3, 2027. We’ll grade it on share-price returns over that period.
