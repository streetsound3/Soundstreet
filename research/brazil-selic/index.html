PREVIEW · EMERGING MARKETS · BRAZIL

The Central Bank of Brazil Will Hold the Selic Rate

November 2, 2026 · Alex Uzzo

Summary. The results are in: the Brazilian presidential election concluded this past Sunday, October 25th, with Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva re-elected as the country’s leader. Lula, a left-leaning politician and member of the Workers’ Party, has historically backed a fiscal policy focus on public investments and gradual deficit decrease. The Central Bank of Brazil meets November 3rd–4th to discuss a monetary policy decision, and I believe they will hold rates.

Our call: The Central Bank of Brazil holds the Selic at 13.75% on Nov 4.

What happened

Lula surprisingly won by a margin of victory of 3.58 percentage points after finishing nearly three percentage points behind Flávio Bolsonaro in the first round earlier this month. Flávio Bolsonaro is still looking to contest the result. Despite his success in the election, Lula is presented with a multitude of challenges as the incumbent president of Brazil. Brazil’s economy has had declining real GDP growth from 3.42% in 2024 to 2.2% in 2025, September annual inflation of 4.58%, and national debt of approximately 82.9% of GDP. To fight inflation, Brazil has a high Selic rate of 13.75% right now, choking out the already slowing economy. The Central Bank of Brazil cut Selic rates five times this year to stimulate the economy and fight national debt, but Lula being elected days before the central bank decision has made it much more complicated. As a result of Lula being elected, the real fell about 8% against the dollar (the dollar closed up 8.79% at R$5.4154), and Brazil’s benchmark market index, the IBOVESPA, fell 13.88% on Monday.

Why it happened

Incumbent president Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva ran on five main policies: protecting the Amazon rainforest, shortening the workweek, raising the minimum wage, increasing social welfare, and an active non-alignment foreign policy. Lula’s success was likely driven by an anti-right movement caused by the belief that Bolsonaro was “excessively radical.” Investors believe Lula has made too many spending promises and will spend more than Brazil can afford, worsening the national debt problem. This caused investors to dump their Brazilian equities, real futures, and the real itself, out of fear of sticky inflation and a debt crisis. Also, the market had already priced in a Bolsonaro victory, so an unexpected Lula victory led to a massive unwinding of speculative trades.

How it flows through

Lula’s election has led to greater expected government spending, deficits, and inflation risks, which could lead the central bank to hold the Selic rate rather than make further cuts due to inflation uncertainty and currency depreciation. This will lead to less borrowing, investment, and consumer demand, ultimately slowing economic growth. A depreciating real will increase import costs, while the central bank’s decision would keep borrowing costs high and weaken consumer demand. These pressures altogether would squeeze profit margins for Brazilian businesses.

Brazilian exporters, on the other hand, could benefit from the effect of a depreciated real. When the real depreciated, it made Brazilian exports cheaper for other countries, leading to increased demand and short-term competitive advantage. Long-term production costs, though, will increase and borrowing costs would stay expensive, decreasing the net benefit of their competitive advantage. The recently popular emerging markets took a big hit from this election, as growing skepticism about Brazil’s economic stability raised concerns about the risk of emerging markets investments as a whole.

The effect on global commodity prices is uncertain, as a weaker real will encourage exports and increase global supply, while rising production costs could limit output and possibly reverse the downward pressure on prices. As Brazilian exports become more competitive in the international markets, foreign producers may struggle to compete, facing potential losses in market share and downward pressure on prices. Imports in Brazil, on the other hand, will become more expensive, reducing sales for trade partners. Any U.S. companies operating in Brazil will also be exposed to exchange rate losses. Ultimately, a weaker real would briefly benefit exporters in Brazil, but higher borrowing, import costs, and inflation would weigh on all Brazilian businesses and their trade partners.

Names to watch

Suzano SUZ

▲ Potential winner

Suzano will benefit from cheaper operating expenses in Brazil because 73% of their expenses are paid in the Brazilian real and the US dollar has appreciated compared to the real. In 2025 they received 85.4% of their net-sales revenue in USD, meaning they have limited exposure to revenue losses due to depreciation. This currency mismatch can improve operating margins, but USD-denominated debt and increased import costs could offset this currency benefit. (Source: Form 20-F, 2025.)

Vale VALE

▲ Potential winner

Vale is a Brazilian iron mining company that reported 47.6% of cost of goods sold denominated in reais in 2025, so they will benefit from the depreciated real and decreased expenses. Iron revenue is priced internationally in USD; therefore, their revenues will not be exposed to a depreciated real. However, foreign-currency debt and iron-ore price fluctuation could offset these benefits. (Source: Form 20-F, 2025.)

SLC Agrícola SLCE3

▲ Potential winner

SLC Agrícola is a soybean and corn exporter on large Brazilian farms. Soybeans and other crops are denominated internationally in dollars, while a portion of its labor and operational expenses are denominated in reais. This could improve their cost competitiveness in international markets. On the other hand, higher prices for dollar-linked import costs could drive up operating expenses, possibly offsetting the other benefits. (Source: Management Report, 2025.)

Although a depreciating real improves the competitiveness of Brazilian exporters, foreign exchange hedging may offset these benefits.

What would prove us wrong

  1. The Central Bank raises rates. This would prove that policymakers believe inflation and fiscal uncertainty pose a bigger threat than we anticipated.
  2. The Central Bank cuts rates. This would indicate that policymakers are more comfortable with the inflation outlook than we anticipated, and that they are more concerned about slow economic growth.
  3. The Brazilian real stabilizes or appreciates significantly before the meeting. This would weaken my argument that the depreciated real will force the central bank to pause rate cuts. It would also reduce imported inflation risks.
  4. Inflation expectations fall significantly and Brazilian national debt concerns ease significantly. This would give the central bank the confidence to continue to cut Selic rates.

Our call (Call #003): The Central Bank of Brazil holds the Selic at 13.75% on November 4, 2026. We’ll grade it against the Central Bank of Brazil’s Copom statement that day.
