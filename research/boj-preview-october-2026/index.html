PREVIEW · JAPAN · CENTRAL BANKS

BOJ preview: the 1.25% hold is priced in. The 2% wording isn't.

October 22, 2026 · Michael Lawton · Decision due October 30

Summary. The Bank of Japan is all but certain to hold its rate at 1.25% on October 30: none of the economists in Bloomberg's survey expect a move. What matters is the wording. The quarterly Outlook Report could say that underlying inflation has reached the 2% target, something the BOJ has not declared since adopting the goal in 2013. Our base case is a hawkish hold: no change to rates, but a higher inflation forecast and wording that points to a hike in December. That would support the yen at the margin, in line with our open Call #001.

The event

The BOJ's policy board meets October 29–30 and publishes its decision with the quarterly Outlook Report, which carries new growth and inflation forecasts.

  • Where rates stand. The BOJ raised its policy rate to 1.25% on September 18, its second hike in three months.
  • What's expected. In Bloomberg's October survey, none of the economists expect a move at this meeting, and 83% expect the next hike in December, up from 35% a month earlier. The median expected peak rate is unchanged at 1.75%. Officials have also signaled little appetite for hikes at back-to-back meetings.
  • Where the yen is. About 157 yen per dollar on October 22, up from about 158 a day earlier, as the 10-year US Treasury yield fell to 5.05%.

Why the wording matters

With the decision priced in, markets will trade the language. Four things set it up.

  • The 2% question. Governor Ueda said on October 19 that underlying inflation is "near 2% target." Reuters sources say the board may slightly raise its fiscal 2027 inflation forecast as higher oil prices feed through to consumer prices.
  • Oil. Brent rose to about $112 a barrel on October 22, its highest since May, on US–Iran tensions. Japan relies heavily on imported energy, so higher oil feeds directly into consumer prices.
  • A split board. September's hike drew two dissents from board members appointed under Prime Minister Takaichi. About two-thirds of economists in the survey think the government has softened its stance on hikes.
  • The Fed goes first. The Federal Reserve decides on October 28, two days earlier. Markets put about 80% odds on a hold at 3.75–4.00%, with a hike widely expected in December. A Fed surprise would move the yen before the BOJ speaks.

One more data point lands before the meeting: September's nationwide inflation figures on October 23, where economists polled by Reuters expect core inflation of 2.1%, up from 1.7% in August.

What we'll be watching on October 30

  1. The 2% line. Whether the Outlook Report says underlying inflation has reached the target, or only that it is approaching it.
  2. The fiscal 2027 forecast. Whether the inflation forecast is revised up. Any upgrade may be small: the planned cut to the sales tax on food, if parliament passes it, would lower measured inflation from April 2027.
  3. The vote. How many members dissent, and in which direction.
  4. Ueda's press conference. Any hint on December, and any comment on the yen.
  5. The yen near 160. Verbal warnings from Japanese officials have capped the yen's slide, and traders see 160 as the level that risks direct intervention. Japan and the US intervened together in late July.

What would prove us wrong

We expect a hawkish hold that keeps December on track. Each of these can be checked on October 30.

  • Wrong on the BOJ if the Outlook Report leaves the fiscal 2027 inflation forecast unchanged or cuts it, or if Ueda declines to keep a December hike on the table.
  • Wrong on the market read if market odds of a December hike fall below 50% by the close on October 30. They were about 73% on October 20.
  • Wrong on the yen if it ends October 30 weaker than 158 yen per dollar despite a hawkish hold.

A Fed surprise on October 28 would not make our BOJ view wrong, but a hike (about 20% odds) would weaken the yen before the BOJ speaks.

Our longer view is tracked as Call #001: the yen will be stronger than 158 yen per dollar on December 18, meaning it takes fewer than 158 yen to buy a dollar. The yen is about 157 today, so the call has little room. A dovish hold that pushes it toward 160, or a Fed hike on December 9 without a BOJ hike to match, would put the call at risk. We will publish a reaction note within 48 hours of the decision.
